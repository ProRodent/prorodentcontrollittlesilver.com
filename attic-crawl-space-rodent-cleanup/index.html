What rodents leave behind, and why it matters.

An attic or crawl space with rodents holds droppings, urine, nesting material and often dead animals. Insulation soaks up urine and carries odor. Dust from dried droppings can be stirred into the air when you walk through, which is the main reason cleanup is not a casual job.

CDC lists salmonellosis, leptospirosis, rat-bite fever and LCMV among rodent-linked concerns, and rodents also contaminate stored food. We do not make medical claims here. If you feel unwell after time in an infested space, talk to a physician.

Cape Cod half-stories, knee walls and eaves.

Cape Cods and mid-century homes are common in the borough, and many have a finished half-story upstairs. Behind the short knee walls sit low, narrow eave spaces packed with insulation. Mice run along them unseen, so droppings collect where no one looks. When we clean, we open the knee-wall access panels and check the full length of each eave, not only the center of the attic.

If you hear scratching at night in an upstairs bedroom, that is often where the animals are. The eave spaces usually connect to the soffits, which is why rodent exclusion and entry point sealing matters before any cleanup begins.

Crawl spaces in a humid, river-adjacent borough.

Little Silver sits at roughly 16 feet of elevation, and lots that back onto a creek or the river tend to have damp ground. Crawl spaces on those lots often run wet, and tidal flooding on low streets can push water into them, so look at yours after a high tide. Wet insulation and vapor barriers stay wet, which helps mold as well as odor.

A crawl space also gives Norway rats a burrow entry along the foundation. We check the vapor barrier for torn or displaced areas, look for tunnels under it, and note any standing water. If moisture is the larger problem, we tell you so, because cleaning without a fix for moisture just resets the clock.

Deodorizing comes after removal, not before. Sprays and scented products mask smell for a day or two, then it returns because the source is still in the framing or the batts. We remove the source, disinfect the wood, and let the space dry. If the crawl space stays damp afterward, a dehumidifier or a fix for the water path is worth discussing with a contractor.

How we clean, step by step.

We follow the CDC approach. The space is ventilated for about 30 minutes before anyone enters. Everyone working wears rubber or vinyl gloves, a respirator, eye protection and disposable coveralls. Dry droppings are never swept or vacuumed with a household vacuum, because that raises dust.

  • Soak droppings and nesting with disinfectant or a mix of 1 part bleach to 9 parts water, and let it sit for 5 minutes.
  • Wipe up with paper towels, then bag and seal the waste and discard it.
  • Remove contaminated insulation into heavy bags without shaking or crushing it.
  • Disinfect wood framing and surfaces, and wash tools and boots before leaving.
  • Wash hands after removing gloves, and put clothing in the wash right away.
Cleaning before the holes are sealed only invites a fresh set of droppings.

The order of work: exclusion first, cleanup second.

If you clean an attic while rodents are still coming in, you will be cleaning it again in a month. We trap first, then seal, then clean, and only then replace insulation. See rodent trapping and removal for the first step of that order.

Some homeowners ask us about chewed wiring during cleanup. Rodents chew wire insulation, and damaged wiring can contribute to a fire. We flag what we see, and rodent damage repair covers the repair side. Many homeowners policies exclude rodent damage, so confirm yours with your agent.

Ask any provider you are considering for their NJDEP pesticide license number, and confirm it with NJDEP before work starts. Cleanup itself uses disinfectants, but some jobs include treatments that fall under those rules.

Before we start.

  • Keep children, pets and anyone with breathing problems out of the attic or crawl space.
  • Do not sweep, vacuum or stir dry droppings, and do not shake old insulation.
  • Clear a path to the attic hatch or crawl space door.
  • Do not cut into insulation yourself if it may be contaminated.
  • Tell us if there is standing water, mold, or an odor that has not faded.

Questions about attic and crawl space rodent cleanup

Does all the insulation have to come out?

No. Insulation with light surface droppings can often be cleaned in place. Batts soaked with urine, nests, and loose-fill full of tunnels usually must be removed. We look at each section and tell you which is which.

Can I clean droppings myself?

You can, if the amount is small and you follow the CDC steps: ventilate, wear gloves, wet the droppings with disinfectant, wipe, bag and wash. For a whole attic, we suggest a professional because of the dust and the volume.

Why does my crawl space smell after rain or a high tide?

Damp conditions bring out urine and mold odor, and low-lying streets here flood at high tide. The smell tells you moisture and contamination are both present. Both need attention.

When can new insulation go in?

After the animals are gone, the entry points are sealed, and the area is clean and dry. Putting new material in early means rodents may ruin it.

Are you available in the evening if I find a lot of droppings?

We answer calls around the clock and can often line up a same-day look, depending on the day's bookings. Until then, stay out of the space and do not disturb the droppings.

Is the bleach mix safe on all surfaces?

The CDC mix is meant for hard surfaces like framing and floors. It can stain fabrics and damage some finishes, so we test a spot first and use a labeled disinfectant where bleach is a poor fit.
