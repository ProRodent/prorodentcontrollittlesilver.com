Read the yard before you read the house.

Rodents often start outdoors. They settle where food, cover and a burrow site are close together, and move inside when the house offers a better deal. Fixing the yard early can stop the problem before it reaches your walls.

About 8% of Little Silver homes sit directly on the Shrewsbury River, and roughly another third sit on streams that connect to it. Norway rats travel along those banks, so if your lot backs onto a creek, start your walk-around on the wet edge of the yard. Burrows and foundation gaps along that edge are the ones most likely to matter.

How to tell what dug that hole.

Not every hole in a yard is a rat burrow. Chipmunks, moles, voles and squirrels all dig, and each needs a different response. Use this table to narrow it down before you buy anything.

The usual yard attractants.

Gardens and compost bins are the most common food source we see. Ripe tomatoes left to rot, fallen fruit under apple or pear trees, and open compost piles with cooked scraps all draw rats. Little Silver's farm and nursery past left fertile soil, and gardens are common. A well-tended vegetable bed can also be a steady meal for a rat, so pick ripe produce promptly and keep beds clear of fallen fruit. Dog food left in a bowl on a patio works the same way as bird seed.

Bird feeders come next. Seed that falls to the ground feeds rodents all night after the birds are gone. Woodpiles stacked against a wall, sheds set on the ground, and low decks give them shelter. A rat that finds food and cover in the same corner tends to stay put.

Sheds and decks deserve a closer look than most people give them. A rat will dig along the edge of a concrete pad or under low deck boards, then line the burrow with shredded paper and fabric. If you can only see a dark gap under the structure, a technician with a flashlight and a mirror can check it in a few minutes.

A yard with no food and no cover is a yard rats move on from.

Changes you can make this weekend.

Yard changes that cut rodent pressure.

  • Use a lidded, rodent-resistant compost bin and keep meat, dairy and cooked food out of it.
  • Pick up fallen fruit and cut back ivy or dense shrubs that touch the foundation.
  • Hang bird feeders over a seed catcher, sweep the ground each evening, or take feeders down while you have active rats.
  • Stack firewood at least a foot off the ground and a foot or more away from the house.
  • Raise sheds and deck skirting so you can see underneath, and close open gaps with hardware cloth of 1/4 inch mesh.
  • Store trash in tight cans with locking lids and do not leave pet food outside overnight.

What treatment outdoors looks like.

We start by locating every active burrow and runway, and we mark where droppings and gnaw marks show up. Rats often have several openings, so plugging a single hole tends to push them to another. We also check the foundation line, deck edges and shed bases to see where they might travel into the house.

Treatment usually combines trapping along runways with tamper-resistant bait stations placed out of reach of children, pets and wildlife. We do not scatter loose bait. Poison can hurt dogs, cats and hawks and owls that eat a poisoned rodent, so placement matters. Once activity stops, burrows are filled and the edges are closed. If the animals were already getting indoors, our rodent exclusion service closes those gaps, and rat control covers how we handle established infestations.

Why you may need a follow-up look.

Rodent numbers near creeks and low ground swing with the seasons and the weather. Heavy rain or a high tide can drive animals out of banks and into yards for a few days. If you saw a burrow after a storm, tell us when it first showed up. Fresh soil and a clean, shiny hole edge usually mean the burrow is in use right now. A few photos of the hole, taken from the side with something for scale, help us judge how fresh it is.

Questions about backyard and outdoor rodent control

Is it a rat burrow or a chipmunk hole?

Rat holes are usually 2 to 3 inches wide with smooth, packed edges and often sit beside a foundation, slab or shed. Chipmunk holes are smaller, tidy and often near stone walls. Rat burrows also tend to have droppings and gnawed scraps close by.

Do you handle rodent activity in a backyard that borders a creek?

Yes. Rats can travel along stream banks, so we check the creek edge of the lot and the fence line first. We also talk through what the neighbors may be doing, since rats do not respect property lines.

Will removing my bird feeder solve the problem?

It helps, but it may not be enough on its own. Feeders are one food source among several. If rats are already burrowing, we will still need to trap or bait, and then fix the food and cover that brought them in.

Can I put out poison from the hardware store myself?

We advise against it. Loose or poorly placed bait can poison pets, wildlife and children, and dying rats may crawl into walls and create odor problems. Consumer bait must come in a tamper-resistant station. Call us if you are unsure what is safe.

Is it a problem if there is a woodpile against the house?

Yes, it gives rodents cover right against the foundation and a short path to any gap. Move it at least a foot off the ground and away from the wall. Stack it neatly so you can see beneath.

Can you come out on short notice if I see a rat in the yard?

Yes, call at any hour. Same-day visits depend on the day's bookings, so ask when you call. Meanwhile, bring pets inside, pick up any food and do not try to corner the animal.

Will my garden be safe if you treat the yard?

We place bait stations and traps away from beds, pets and play areas, and we tell you where they are. Ask us on the call about vegetable gardens so we can choose the safest layout.
