Rats Under a Shed or Deck: Burrow Signs and Fixes

In a borough of gardens, mulch beds and stream-side lots, the dry space under a shed or deck is prime real estate for Norway rats.

How can I tell if rats are living under my shed or deck?

Look for holes about two to four inches wide with smooth, packed edges, set along the base of the shed, deck posts or foundation. Fresh dirt piled at the opening, worn paths in the grass and a lack of cobwebs are the clearest signs of a burrow that is in use. Norway rats dig these and use them for nesting and shelter.

A single hole does not tell the whole story. Rats usually have several entrances and a network of tunnels, so one visible hole often means more you cannot see. Walk the full edge of the structure, and check where the shed meets a fence, a wall or a garden bed, since rats like to travel along edges.

SignActive burrowAbandoned burrow
Entrance edgesSmooth, packed and polished from trafficCrumbling, dry and rough.
Webs and debrisClear of cobwebs and leavesCobwebs, leaf litter or plant growth across the hole.
DirtFresh, loose dirt fanned out at the mouthOld, crusted or rain-flattened soil.
RunwaysBare, greasy paths that lead to the holeGrass and weeds growing over the path.
DroppingsFresh, dark and about half an inch longOld, gray and crumbly, or none.

If you are unsure, put a loose plug of crumpled paper or a handful of soil in the hole and check it the next day. If it has been pushed out or dug open, the burrow is active. That simple test takes a minute and tells you whether you need to act. Repeat it once more two days later if the first result is unclear.

What draws rats into a backyard in the first place?

Food, water and cover. A shed or deck supplies cover, and the yard usually supplies the rest. Rats do not need much, and the leftovers in a typical yard add up quickly.

  • Bird seed on the ground beneath feeders, a common food source.
  • Open compost piles or bins with loose lids, especially when they hold meat, bread or cooked food.
  • Pet food left outside overnight, and dog waste that is not picked up.
  • Fallen fruit and nuts from trees and garden beds that no one gathers.
  • Clutter such as stacked lumber, tarps, pots and firewood piled against a wall.

Gardens are common in Little Silver, and vegetable beds, heavy mulch and dense plantings also give rats cover and food. Keep mulch thin near the house, pull it back from the shed and deck, and harvest ripe produce promptly.

Why are stream-side and waterfront lots a bigger risk?

Rats travel along banks, culverts and drainage ditches, so the edge of a yard that meets a stream or the river is the first place they arrive. If your lot is one of these, look along the yard edge nearest the water before any other.

High water pushes them up as well. After a storm or a big tide, rats that lived along the bank move into higher, drier spots like the space under a deck. If your yard floods, look again a few days later. Our post on Norway rats in waterfront homes covers more of what to expect there.

Rats dig where it is dry, sheltered and close to food. Change any one of those, and they move on.

What can I fix myself?

Homeowner fixes work best when they cut off food and shelter together. Removing food alone does not make the burrow go away. Blocking the burrow alone, without cutting off food, sends rats to dig a new one nearby. Plan on doing both.

Weekend fix list.

  • Pick up bird seed daily, or switch to a feeder with a tray that catches the hulls.
  • Store pet food and birdseed in metal or heavy plastic containers with tight lids.
  • Use a closed compost bin with a solid bottom, and keep meat and grease out of it.
  • Move firewood, lumber and pots at least a foot off the ground and away from the wall.
  • Cut back dense shrubs and ground cover that touch the shed or deck.
  • Fill or block old holes only after you are sure no animal is inside.

For the shed itself, one of the more lasting fixes is to raise it. A shed on a gravel pad or blocks, with open air beneath, gives rats little to hide behind. If you cannot raise it, add a skirt of hardware cloth with quarter inch mesh. A typical approach is to bury it about a foot deep and bend the bottom outward in an L shape, so digging rats hit metal.

Decks need their own approach. Clear stored items from underneath, keep the ground there bare so rats have no cover, and close the perimeter with hardware cloth before digging starts. Leave one section you can lift or open, so that you can shine a flashlight under the deck each month. Check again after heavy rain, since washed-out soil can expose a new hole.

When should I call a professional, and what happens?

Call when you see several burrows, when droppings show up near the house, when you hear noises in walls or the attic, or when your own fixes have not worked. Burrows near a foundation can lead into a basement or crawl space. Our rat control visits start with an inspection of the whole yard and structure.

A technician looks at the burrow pattern, the food sources and the ways in. Where bait is appropriate, we use tamper-resistant bait stations, placed out of reach of children, pets and wildlife and set as the label requires. We check the burrows over the following visits to see whether activity drops.

Our backyard and outdoor rodent control work pairs treatment with sanitation advice, and rodent exclusion closes the entry points on the house.

How do I keep pets and wildlife safe?

Loose poison is a real risk to dogs, cats, children and wildlife. Second-generation anticoagulant products can also poison a hawk, owl or pet that eats a poisoned rat, which is called secondary poisoning. That is why the rules require tamper-resistant stations and why we do not scatter bait in the open.

If you have a dog, keep it away from burrows and bait stations, and do not let it dig or roll in the spot. If a pet has eaten something you suspect is rodent poison, call your veterinarian right away and bring the product label. Do not pour bleach, gasoline or other chemicals into a burrow, because they harm soil, pets and neighboring wildlife.

Common questions

Who do I call if a burrow opens beside my back door on a Sunday?

Our 24/7 line takes calls at any hour, and we schedule same-day visits where our calendar allows. Rats seen in daylight, or a burrow next to a door, are reasons to call right away.

Is filling in the burrow enough to get rid of them?

No. Rats will dig around a filled hole or open a new one nearby. Filling works only after the animals are gone and the food and shelter are dealt with. Otherwise you have moved the problem a few feet.

Will rats from under the shed get into my house?

They can. Burrows along a foundation can lead to gaps in the wall, a crawl space door or a basement window. Rats only need a gap of about one half inch, so check the house edge as carefully as the shed.

How can I tell rats from mice outside?

Rat burrows are wider, about two to four inches, with worn entrances. Rat droppings are about half an inch to three quarters of an inch and blunt, while mouse droppings are smaller and pointed. Our mouse control page covers the smaller animals.

Should I use poison from the hardware store myself?

We advise against it. Consumer products must be sold in tamper-resistant stations, but placement mistakes still put pets and wildlife at risk. A professional follows the label and monitors results.
