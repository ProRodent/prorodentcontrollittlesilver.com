Kitchens, chases and wall voids: where mice live in older Little Silver homes.

The median home in Little Silver was built roughly in 1956, and Cape Cods and split-levels from that era are common here. Houses of that age often have open gaps where pipes and wires pass through framing, and those gaps connect wall voids to the kitchen and the basement or crawl space. A mouse that gets into one void can travel to every room without ever crossing an open floor.

So we start in the kitchen. Pull out the cabinet under the sink and look at the back panel: the round cuts around supply lines and drain pipes are a very common mouse doorway in a house of this age. Fresh gnaw marks or dark, greasy rub marks at those cuts tell us the void behind the wall is in use.

House mouse or white-footed mouse.

Two species get into homes in our area, and the difference changes where we place traps and what we seal. House mice live with people all year. White-footed mice come from woods and hedges, and they show up more often in sheds, garages and porches near heavy landscaping.

If you are unsure which one you have, do not guess from a single glimpse. We look at droppings, rub marks and entry points during the visit and match the plan to the animal.

Why a few mice become dozens.

Indoors, mice breed all year. Gestation is about 19 to 21 days, and a female can have 5 to 10 litters a year, with around 5 or 6 pups in each. That means a pair you hear in November can be a crowded colony by spring.

For that reason, a few snap traps from the hardware store rarely finish the job. They remove the mice you catch, but the unsealed gap keeps letting new ones in, and the ones already breeding in the wall keep producing. Speed matters, and so does a plan that cuts off the supply.

Treatment plus exclusion: the two halves of mouse control.

Treatment removes the mice that are already inside. Exclusion closes the ways in. Either half alone fails: trapping without sealing turns into a monthly routine, and sealing without trapping locks live mice inside with your food and your wiring.

  • We inspect inside and out, mark every gap of 1/4 inch or more, and note where droppings and rub marks concentrate.
  • We set snap traps and, where the site allows it, tamper-resistant stations in the spots mice actually travel, usually along walls and behind appliances.
  • We seal gaps with materials mice cannot chew through, such as hardware cloth, sheet metal, copper mesh and mortar. Steel wool alone does not hold, so we do not rely on it.
  • We return to check catches, remove traps and confirm that activity has stopped before the work is closed.

The sealing step is its own service when a house has many entry points, and you can read how it works on our rodent exclusion page. If we need to look at a garage or basement that connects to the living space, our garage and basement rodent control service covers those areas.

Quiet houses near the train station.

Little Silver has a commuter rhythm. The 1890 station, designed by Henry Hobson Richardson, sends many households to New York on weekday mornings, and many commuter households leave the house empty and quiet from early morning until evening. Mice tend to be more active where it is quiet, so a home like that can carry weeks of activity before anyone notices.

If that describes your schedule, check the signs on a weekend morning. Look for droppings along baseboards and in the back of the pantry, and listen at dusk when the house is still. Tell us your daily pattern on the call, because it helps us decide when to set traps and when to return.

Do this before we arrive.

  • Store pantry food, pet food and birdseed in hard containers with tight lids.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum droppings. Ventilate the room for 30 minutes, wear gloves, and wet them with disinfectant first.
  • Do not scatter loose poison. It can kill mice inside walls, where the smell lasts for weeks, and it puts pets at risk.
  • Note where you heard or saw activity, and what time of day.
  • Keep the cabinet under the kitchen sink empty so we can see the back panel.

Questions about mouse control

How do I know it is mice and not rats?

Mouse droppings are small and pointed, about 1/8 to 1/4 inch. Rat droppings are closer to 1/2 to 3/4 inch and blunt. Mice also leave small, scattered rub marks along baseboards, while rats tend to leave larger gnaw holes and burrows outside.

Can I just use glue boards or poison?

We do not recommend loose poison in a house with pets or children, and glue boards catch other animals and often leave mice alive for hours. Snap traps and tamper-resistant stations placed on travel routes work better, and sealing the gaps keeps new mice out.

Can I call after hours about mice?

Yes, the line is open around the clock. If mice are in a bedroom or chewing wiring, say so first so we can flag the call as urgent. A same-day visit depends on the schedule.

How long until the mice are gone?

It depends on the number of mice and how many entry points the house has. Small problems can sometimes settle in days, while an established colony in a wall void can take a few visits. We check catches and activity before closing the work.

Why do mice seem worse in fall?

Cooling nights in September to November push mice toward warm buildings. Homes with gaps around utility lines, garage doors and foundations see the first arrivals, so sealing before the cold is the best prevention.

Are white-footed mice a real problem in Little Silver homes?

They can be, especially in sheds, garages and porches close to hedges and gardens. They nest in stored items and firewood, so we look at outbuildings and cut-back cover as well as the house itself.

My house is empty all day because we commute. Does that matter?

It can. Mice tend to be more active in quiet houses, and traps may catch more when nobody is moving around. Tell us your schedule and we will plan the visit and the trap checks around it.
