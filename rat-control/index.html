Rats and the Shrewsbury River: what the water means for your yard.

About 8% of Little Silver homes sit directly on the Shrewsbury River, and roughly a third more sit on streams that connect to it. Norway rats travel along banks and drainage edges, so the first place they find a gap is usually the side of a house that faces the water. If your property is on or near a stream, walk that side first and look for holes and worn runways.

Public access points matter too. The public boat launch at Dominick F. Santelle Park on Riverview Avenue sits on the Shrewsbury River, and waterfront edges are where rats travel. If you live within a few blocks of Riverview Avenue, keep bins lidded and pick up fallen bird seed, because food left outside gives rats a reason to leave the bank.

Norway rat or roof rat.

In New Jersey the rat you are almost certainly dealing with is the Norway rat. Roof rats are uncommon here, so if someone tells you they are in your attic, ask what evidence they saw before accepting the diagnosis.

Signs of a burrow before you see a rat.

Norway rat burrows are usually a hole about 2 to 3 inches wide, with smooth, packed soil around it and no spider webs across the opening. Look along foundations, beside steps, under decks and sheds, and beneath any slab. A burrow with dirt kicked out in a fan and a worn path in the grass is in active use.

Rats also leave trails. They use the same route night after night, so a strip of flattened grass or a smudge along the base of a wall shows where they travel. If you can find that path, we can place traps and stations on it instead of guessing.

  • Gnaw marks on wood, plastic or pipe insulation, larger than what a mouse leaves.
  • Greasy rub marks along walls and around a hole where fur and body oils rub off.
  • Droppings about 1/2 to 3/4 inch long, blunt on the ends, near food or under a deck.
  • Scratching or thumping in the ceiling or walls at night, usually heavier than a mouse.
A rat only needs a hole about 1/2 inch wide to get in.

How we treat rats: burrows, bait stations, then sealing.

We begin by mapping burrows and entry points, then decide on traps, bait stations or both. Snap traps are strong enough for rats and are set out of reach of pets. Where a bait station is the right choice, we use a tamper-resistant model, placed along runways and fixed so children, pets and wildlife cannot reach the bait.

That last point is a rule, not a preference. Under EPA rules for second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides, consumer products must be sold in tamper-resistant stations, and professionals follow the label on placement. A dog or a hawk that eats a poisoned rat can be poisoned too, which is why we do not scatter loose bait.

Pets, wildlife and what to keep away from bait.

If you have a dog that digs, tell us. We place stations where a dog cannot reach them, and we favor traps in yards with wandering animals. Do not spread grain-based poison yourself, and do not move a bait station once it is set.

Wildlife is the second concern. Hawks, owls and foxes eat poisoned rodents, and that is how bait travels up the food chain. It is one reason we prefer traps near open ground, and use stations only where the bait cannot reach other animals.

Close the burrow and the entry after the rats are gone.

Rats reuse old burrows, so filling a hole without treating the colony only pushes them to dig a new one. Once catches drop, we fill or barrier the burrow, and we seal foundation gaps with cement, sheet metal or hardware cloth. If the rats got in through a crawl space or attic, our attic and crawl space cleanup service handles the droppings and insulation left behind.

When the activity is in a yard, shed or deck, our backyard and outdoor rodent control work covers the outside half. If you want to see how we plan a visit first, the rodent inspection page walks through it.

What you can do before the visit.

  • Walk the water-facing side of your house and note any holes, runways or dug-out soil.
  • Take down or clean up bird feeders, and pick up fallen seed and pet food each night.
  • Keep trash bins lidded and off the ground if you can.
  • Do not pour bleach or gasoline into a burrow, and do not stuff it with rags.
  • Keep pets and children away from any dead rodent, and tell us if one is found.

Questions about rat control

Are roof rats a concern in Little Silver?

Not usually. Roof rats are uncommon in New Jersey, and most rats in coastal homes are Norway rats. We identify the species from droppings, burrows and gnaw marks before choosing treatment.

Are bait stations safe with a dog or cat?

Tamper-resistant stations are built so pets cannot reach the bait, and we place them out of pet and wildlife reach. A rat that ate bait can still be a risk if a pet catches it, so we often use snap traps in yards with pets.

Can you help if I am on a stream, not the river itself?

Yes. About a third of homes here sit on streams that connect to the Shrewsbury River, and rats follow those banks. We inspect the water-facing side and the foundation the same way.

Can I call about a rat after dark?

Yes. The phone line runs all night, and a same-day visit is often possible. If a rat is inside your living space or a bedroom, say so when you call.

What does a rat burrow look like?

It is a hole about 2 to 3 inches across in packed soil, often beside a foundation, step, deck or shed, with a worn path leading to it. Fresh dirt around the hole means it is active.

Will filling the burrow get rid of the rats?

No. Rats dig a new hole nearby, or push through a weak spot in the foundation. We treat the colony first and close the burrow after catches stop.

Should I handle a dead rat myself?

Wear gloves, avoid touching it with bare hands, and double bag it. If you are unsure, call us, and talk to a physician if you were bitten or had contact with droppings.
