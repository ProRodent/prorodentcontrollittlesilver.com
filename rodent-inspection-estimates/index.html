What happens during a rodent inspection.

A technician walks the outside first, then the inside, then the spaces in between. Outside means the foundation, vents, utility lines, roofline, decks and any burrows. Inside means kitchen cabinets, basement or crawl space, attic and garage. We are looking for three things: signs of activity, the entry points, and the conditions that keep drawing rodents in.

Where the house allows it, we also look at what is outside the walls. Firewood stacked against siding, a shed close to the foundation, or a low branch touching the roof gives rodents a path, and moving those items is often the easiest fix a homeowner can make. We point these out on the walk-through so you can act on them the same day.

We use a flashlight and an inspection mirror to look into voids without tearing anything open. Droppings, rub marks, gnaw holes and nesting material tell us the species and how long the activity has been going on. At the end, we show you what we found and where, and we photograph the entry points so you can show them to anyone doing repairs.

We also ask about the timeline. When did you first hear or see something, does it happen at a set hour, and did anything change, such as a storm, a renovation or a new bird feeder? Those answers often point to the entry point faster than searching alone. If you have noticed a change in the weather, a new appliance or work by a contractor, mention that as well, since each can open a new gap.

Why the age of your house changes the inspection.

Little Silver's oldest surviving dwelling is the Parker Homestead, an early 1700s house on the National Register of Historic Places. Homes from the early 1700s onward tend to have stone or brick foundations with mortar gaps, low crawl spaces and irregular framing. In those houses we spend more time on the foundation, sill and chimney base, because rodents use gaps in stone and mortar that a modern house does not have.

Mid-century Cape Cods and split-levels.

Mid-century Cape Cods and split-levels are common in Little Silver. These often have wall voids that connect a crawl space or basement to the kitchen, and open cutouts behind cabinets. We check the back panels under sinks and the wall behind the water heater, since those cutouts are where mice usually climb into the living space.

Newer rebuilds are not automatically tight.

A newly built house still has utility lines, dryer vents and roof edges that can be left with gaps. Foam that was sprayed around a pipe can be chewed away in a season. So we test each penetration, and we do not assume new construction is sealed.

When a rebuild replaces an older house, the soil and the neighboring yards can still hold the same rodents. New walls do not remove that pressure, so the outside perimeter check matters as much as the inside. Plantings, fences and shared property lines get checked for the same reason.

The inspection decides the plan. A rushed look gives you a plan that misses the entry point.

How the estimate is built after the inspection.

We do not name a figure over the phone, because the work depends on what we find. After the inspection we add up the parts of the job: how many entry points need sealing, what materials each gap needs, how many trap or station visits the activity calls for, and whether cleanup or repair is involved. Each part is listed so you can see what it covers.

  • Entry points: the number of gaps, where they are, and whether each needs hardware cloth, sheet metal, mortar or sealant.
  • Treatment: the number of traps or stations and how many return visits are expected.
  • Access: whether the work is in a crawl space, a tall attic or an occupied kitchen, which changes the time needed.
  • Add-ons: cleanup of droppings and insulation, odor control, and any damage repair that is needed.

If the findings point to a bigger job, we explain which step is first. For example, a house with many gaps often starts with rodent exclusion, while one with live activity in the walls starts with trapping and removal.

Before the technician arrives.

  • Clear access to the under-sink cabinet, the basement or crawl space hatch, and the attic entry.
  • Make a short list of where you have seen droppings, heard noise or found chewing.
  • Put pets in a room away from the areas being checked.
  • Leave droppings where they are so we can read the pattern.
  • Gather any earlier pest reports or repair records for the house.

Questions about rodent inspection and estimates

How long does an inspection take?

An hour or a little more is common, depending on size and how many spaces we need to see. Attics, crawl spaces and older stone foundations take longer, so tell us the age and layout of your house on the call.

Will you tell me the price on the phone?

No. The work depends on how many entry points there are and what treatment is needed, so we build the estimate after the inspection and go through each part with you.

Does the age of my house matter?

Yes. Homes from the early 1700s onward have foundations and framing that hide rodents differently from a mid-century Cape Cod or a recent rebuild, so we change where we look first.

Do you come out for an inspection at night or on a weekend?

Someone answers the line 24/7, and same-day inspections are possible on some days. If the problem is urgent, such as a rodent in a bedroom, say so and we will treat it as a priority.

Do I need to be home?

Someone should be there to give access and hear the findings. If you are a commuter, tell us and we will try to find a time that works.

What if you find no rodents?

We tell you that plainly. We will still note any gaps and conditions that could let rodents in later, so you know what to watch for.
