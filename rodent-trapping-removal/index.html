Why trapping comes before anything else.

Every rodent already inside your walls, attic or basement has to be removed before any hole is closed. Trapping does that job. It also gives you proof: a dead animal in a trap tells us the species, the size, and where it was travelling.

Mice and rats behave differently, so the trap and the placement change with the animal. A mouse explores a few feet from its nest and checks every new object slowly. A Norway rat avoids new objects for days and sticks to a single route. If you are not sure which one you have, rat control and mouse control explain the signs.

Beds, mulch and old nursery soil: where borough rodents start.

Little Silver grew from farms and marshes, and Lovett's Nursery, once described as the East Coast's largest, was part of that landscaping tradition. The legacy is fertile soil and many gardens, and mulch beds often sit right against foundations. Mulch holds warmth and hides movement, so rodents can travel under it and slip into any gap in the wall behind it.

That changes where we look. We start with the plant beds along the foundation, not the middle of the yard. Rub marks, runways in the mulch and small burrow openings beside a shrub tell us where to set traps. Pulling mulch back about a foot from the wall is a simple step you can take today.

Where traps go, and why placement matters more than the trap.

Rodents keep a shoulder or whisker to a vertical surface as they move. We set traps flat against walls, with the trigger end toward the wall and the trap in the path, not beside it. Good spots include behind appliances, the corners of the basement, along joists, and on both sides of a suspected entry hole.

  • Set traps in pairs, at right angles to each other, along a runway with droppings or grease marks.
  • Place cover boxes over rat traps so the animal sees a dark tunnel and not a bare device.
  • Do not scatter a dozen traps across an open floor, because rodents rarely cross open space.
  • Use only a pea-sized bit of peanut butter or a few oats on mouse traps, since too much food lets the mouse lick without triggering.
  • Leave a trap unset with bait for a day or two in the case of a cautious rat, so it treats the box as normal.
A trap set in the wrong place catches nothing, however good it is.

Checks and a monitoring schedule.

Traps are useless if nobody looks at them. We plan repeat visits during the first week and then space them out, because a live population often shows up in the first few nights and slows as the numbers fall. Between visits, each trap is inspected and reset, and a catch is logged by location.

When the catches stop for several visits in a row, we treat that as a sign the indoor population is gone, not as proof. The next step is sealing the entry points so nothing new arrives, which is covered in rodent exclusion and entry point sealing. Closing holes before the last animal is caught can trap it inside.

Why bait alone is a poor first move.

Poison bait works slowly. A poisoned rodent can wander into a wall void, die there, and leave a smell that lasts roughly one to three weeks. Loose bait can also be eaten by pets, and secondary poisoning of pets and hawks or owls is a documented risk. EPA rules also require consumer products with second-generation anticoagulants to come in tamper-resistant stations.

If a bait station has a role, it is as a second tool outside the home, set by a professional according to the label and away from children, pets and wildlife. Indoors, a snap trap gives you a body you can see and remove. If a rodent has already died in a wall, our dead rodent removal and odor control service handles that separate problem.

Safe disposal and what to do with a catch.

Never handle a dead rodent with bare hands. We wear gloves, place the animal and trap contents in a sealed bag, and discard it with household trash, then wash our hands. We wet the area with disinfectant instead of sweeping, which follows CDC guidance for droppings and urine. CDC lists salmonellosis, leptospirosis and rat-bite fever among rodent-linked concerns, so if you feel unwell after exposure, talk to a physician.

Prep for trap placement.

  • Pull mulch and leaf litter back about a foot from the foundation on the side where you hear or see activity.
  • Put away pet food and bird seed in sealed metal or hard plastic bins.
  • Do not put out loose poison, which can kill a pet or leave a carcass inside a wall.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum droppings, since that puts dust in the air. Leave them for us or follow CDC wet-cleaning steps.
  • Note where you heard noise and at what hour, so we set traps along the right route.

Questions about rodent trapping and removal

How many days before traps start catching?

Mice often trigger a trap within the first two or three nights. Rats are more cautious and may take several days to approach a new device. That is why we set traps unarmed at first in some rat cases.

Can I use the traps from a hardware store myself?

You can, and a basic snap trap is a fair start for one mouse. The difficulty is placement and follow-up, and a single trap in the wrong spot teaches the animal to avoid that area. Call us if you find droppings in more than one room.

Are the traps safe around my dog or cat?

Enclosed boxes keep pets and children away from the trigger. We place every device out of reach where possible and tell you where each one is. Tell us on the call what pets are in the home.

What if I hear noises but no trap catches anything?

Then the animal may be using a different route, or the noise may come from a wall or attic space we have not reached. We move the traps and check for a second entry point, and we compare the pattern with what an inspection shows.

Can I call while I can hear the animal moving?

Yes, the line is open 24/7. Tell us where the sound is coming from, and we will book a same-day visit if the schedule has room.

What happens to the rodent after it is caught?

We bag it in a sealed bag and dispose of it with household trash. We do not release rodents, because a released rat or mouse can come back or settle at a neighbor's house.
