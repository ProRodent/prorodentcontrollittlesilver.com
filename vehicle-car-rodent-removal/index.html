Cars that sit still are cars mice move into.

Some Little Silver drivers take the train into New York. Their cars sit in a driveway overnight, then in station parking for a full workday. A nest can build up over a workweek and show no sign until the weekend. A parked vehicle is a sheltered, undisturbed space, and an engine that is still warm after a drive can draw mice on cool nights.

Fall is the peak season, when cooler nights push mice toward warmth, and cold snaps from December to February keep them looking. A car parked next to a garden, a woodpile or a shed is easier for mice to reach than one parked in an open driveway. Cars in a garage that already has rodents are at higher risk.

Check before you turn the key.

A two-minute check for a car that has been parked a while.

  • Pop the hood and look for debris, droppings or stuffed material around the battery, air intake and top of the engine.
  • Check the cabin air filter cover if you can reach it, and smell for a musty or urine odor from the vents.
  • Open the glove box and console and look for food stashes or shredded paper.
  • Wear rubber or vinyl gloves and a mask, and open the doors to air out the car for about 30 minutes before you sit in it.
  • If you find a nest or chewed wiring, stop and do not start the engine.

What not to do.

Do not start the engine to see if it runs. A nest in the engine bay can sit against hot exhaust parts, and a chewed wire can short out. Turning the key can start a fire or turn a small repair into a large one.

Do not vacuum or sweep dry droppings. That puts dust in the air you breathe. Wet the area with disinfectant or a mix of 1 part bleach to 9 parts water, let it sit for 5 minutes, wipe with paper towels and bag everything. Do not use loose poison in or under the car. An animal that dies inside a vent or under the dash can leave an odor that lasts for weeks, and a pet can find the bait.

If you find a nest, stop, gloves on, keys out of the ignition.

How we remove rodents from a vehicle.

We inspect the engine bay, cabin air filter housing, trunk and interior. Nests and droppings are removed with protective gear and disinfected, and traps are set where we see runways, usually in the garage or driveway rather than inside the car. We also look at where the car is parked, since the source is often a nest in the garage or a burrow near the driveway.

If your garage is the source, our garage and basement rodent control service treats the space and closes the gaps. Yard sources such as bird seed spills or a woodpile beside the driveway are covered by our backyard and outdoor rodent control work. We treat the rodents, and we do not repair the vehicle.

Garage versus driveway: keeping them out.

A tightly closed garage protects a car best, but only if it is clean. Keep pet food and seed in sealed bins, close gaps at the door, and do not stack cardboard around the car. In a driveway, park away from the shed, hedge and woodpile when you can, and check under the hood every week or two in fall and winter.

Some drivers use peppermint oil sachets or ultrasonic devices. Evidence that they work well is thin, and a mouse that is hungry or cold may ignore them. Repellent claims on a package are no substitute for removing food and sealing gaps. The steady fixes are removing food, sealing gaps and keeping a nearby population under control.

When it becomes a mechanic's job.

If wiring is chewed, hoses are gnawed, or insulation under the hood is torn, have a qualified mechanic inspect it before you drive. We can clear the animals and the nest, but we do not do vehicle repairs. Insurance is worth a call: comprehensive coverage on an auto policy may cover rodent damage, but this varies, so review the animal-damage terms in your policy or ask your agent. Take photos before cleanup for your records.

Questions about vehicle and car rodent removal

Can I drive my car if I found droppings but no nest?

It is safer to have the engine bay and wiring looked at first. Droppings mean a mouse has been there, and it may have chewed something you cannot see. Air out the car, wear gloves and call us before you turn the key.

Do mice really chew car wiring?

Yes. Rodents gnaw constantly to keep their teeth worn down, and wire insulation is a common target. Damaged wiring can contribute to electrical faults and, in bad cases, fires, so have any chewing checked.

I park at the train station all day. Can mice get into the car there?

They can. Any car parked for a long stretch beside grass, hedges or a trash area is at risk. Check under the hood, look for debris and open the glove box when you come home, especially in fall.

Do you come out at night or on weekends for a car problem?

Yes, the line is answered at all hours, and we can often fit in a same-day visit. If you find a nest at 6 a.m. before a commute, call and we will advise you on the next step.

Will my insurance cover rodent damage to my car?

It depends. Comprehensive coverage on some auto policies covers animal damage, and others exclude it or apply a deductible. Your agent can tell you how your policy treats animal damage, and photos of the damage will help.

How do I get rid of the smell in my car?

Remove the source first: the nest, food stash and droppings. Then disinfect hard surfaces and replace the cabin air filter if it was involved. If an animal died in the car, the smell can last a week or more, and our dead rodent removal and odor control service can help.

Do mothballs or peppermint oil work?

Not reliably. Mothballs are a pesticide that should not be used in a car, and peppermint fades quickly. Removing food, closing garage gaps and controlling nearby rodents works better.
